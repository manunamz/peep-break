    They're feathered out

      Brooding guide

      Watch where they sleep

      The chicks tell you if the heat is right. A thermometer at chick height helps, but their behaviour is the real gauge.

      • Too cold: packed tightly under the lamp, loud and constant peeping. Cold chicks can pile on each other and smother.
      • Too hot: spread out to the far edges, panting with beaks open, wings held away from the body.
      • About right: spread through the space, eating, drinking and napping, with soft contented trills.

      Chicks nap a lot and often fall asleep mid-step with their beak on the floor. That is normal. A chick standing hunched and alone, ruffled and not eating, is worth a closer look.

      Warm under the lamp, cool elsewhere

      Start at 32 to 35 °C (90 to 95 °F) at chick height under the heat source, then lower it by about 3 °C (5 °F) each week. Stop adding heat at around 21 °C (70 °F), or when the chicks are fully feathered at about six weeks.

      Always leave a cooler area so chicks can move away from the heat. Raising a lamp lowers the temperature beneath it.

      Many guides suggest roughly one 250-watt heat lamp per 50 chicks in a cool building, and more when it is cold. Heat lamps are a common cause of barn and coop fires. Hang them from a chain or wire, never only the clamp, keep them well clear of bedding, and check the fittings. Radiant heating plates are a safer alternative.

      Chick starter, all the time

      Feed a chick starter crumble with about 18–20% protein from day one and keep it available at all times. For the first day or two, sprinkle some on a paper towel so the chicks find it.

      • Medicated starter (usually amprolium) helps protect against coccidiosis. Skip it if your chicks were vaccinated for coccidiosis.
      • Never feed layer feed to chicks. Its calcium level can damage growing kidneys.
      • Keep treats under about 10% of their diet. If they eat anything other than starter, give them chick grit to help digest it.

      Fresh, shallow, and clean

      Chicks need clean water at all times. Dehydration kills young chicks faster than hunger does.

      • When chicks arrive, dip each beak gently in the water so they learn where it is.
      • Chicks can drown in deep water. Use chick waterers, and put marbles or pebbles in the trough for the first days.
      • Shavings and droppings get kicked into the water fast. Refresh it at least daily, and raise the waterer on a block as they grow.

      Pine shavings and fresh air

      Pine shavings are absorbent and soft. Avoid cedar, whose aromatic oils irritate the airways. Avoid slick surfaces like newspaper, which can cause splayed legs. Paper towels over the shavings for the first few days stop chicks eating the bedding while they learn what food is.

      Spot-clean wet patches and droppings, especially around the waterers, and add fresh shavings often. A smell of ammonia means the bedding is overdue. Ammonia damages young lungs.

      Ventilate the room, but avoid drafts at chick level. Opening a window helps on warm days. On cold nights it steals the heat the lamps are working to provide.

      Pasty butt and other trouble

      Pasty butt is when droppings dry over the vent and seal it shut. It is most common in the first week, often after stress from shipping or bad temperatures, and it can kill a chick within a day or two. Check vents daily. Soften the mess with a warm damp cloth and ease it off gently. Never pull it away dry.

      Coccidiosis shows up as lethargy, ruffled feathers, hunched posture, and sometimes bloody droppings. Talk to a vet or your feed store quickly. Clean dry bedding and medicated starter both help prevent it.

      Losses happen. Even with good care, a few chicks may not make it through the first week. Several deaths in a short time usually point to heat, water or illness, so check those first.

      Space: plan on about 0.05 m² (half a square foot) per chick at first and around 0.2 m² (2 square feet) by six weeks. Round off brooder corners with cardboard so frightened or cold chicks can't pile into them.

      How this barn works

      Time runs seven times faster here: one real day is one week of brooding. Feed and water are the exception: like a real brooder, they need checking about twice a day in Care mode.

      • Heat: each lamp warms about 50 chicks. Stand under a lamp and press E to raise it or Q to lower it. Grab a spare from the lamp crate to hang more.
      • Feed and water: scoop starter from the sacks by the door and fill a pail at the pump on the right-hand wall.
      • Bedding: grab pine shavings from the wrapped bale on the left wall and spread them over droppings.
      • Health: look for a hunched chick standing alone, pick it up, and clean its vent.
      • Goal: in Care mode, raise as many as you can until they're feathered out at week six.
